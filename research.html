Discriminating Theories of Visual Cortex by Their Prohibitions

A prohibition-based method with a three-model implementation

The problem

Many different mechanisms can produce the same behaviour. In one small, fully characterised circuit, parameter sets differing by factors of two to twenty gave essentially the same network output (Prinz, Bucher & Marder, 2004). So when a model fits neural data, that shows the model is sufficient, not that it is actual. A working model is an existence proof, not a confirmation.

The idea

Compare theories by what each one forbids, not by what it predicts. A confirmed prediction is consistent with many alternatives, but one violated prohibition is enough to rule a theory out. This builds on the tradition of strong inference (Platt, 1964) and adds an explicit scale of evidential strength.

A scale of evidential strength

Every result is classified by the level of evidence it reaches. A behavioural match is the weakest and says nothing about mechanism.

1Behavioural matchweakest
2Match in internal representation
3Match in a named signature, feature by feature
4Effect of direct intervention on the mechanism
5Direct observation of a prohibited eventstrongest

Three models, chosen to differ on what the theories dispute

Model A

Sparse coding

Dictionary learning on natural-image patches. Produces oriented, localised receptive fields with no hierarchy and no prediction.

Model B

Feedforward feature detection

A convolutional network that maps input to category, with no generative component.

Model C

Predictive coding

A network with explicit error units and reciprocal predictions, built with an existing software library.

Procedure

  1. Narrow the candidate mechanisms to those consistent with the data, using simulation and identifiability analysis.
  2. Locate where the surviving mechanisms diverge, by comparing their representations.
  3. Resolve the divergence by perturbation. Mechanisms that look the same under passive observation often separate under intervention.

Model recovery first

Before any comparison, synthetic data are generated from each model, all three models are fitted to each dataset, and the confusion matrix checks that the models can be told apart. Comparisons use encoding models with variance partitioning, crediting each model only with the variance it explains uniquely. The metric is fixed before fitting.

Scope

This phase is a comparison among models, not a test against the brain. It produces located, testable divergences and a first candidate discriminating experiment. Testing them against neural recordings (Allen Brain Observatory, OpenScope) is a later phase.

Paper in preparation: Hasan, R. U. Discriminating Theories of Visual Cortex by Their Prohibitions: A Prohibition-Based Method with a Three-Model Implementation.

Detecting Gradual, Coordinated Physiological Drift

Person-specific sequential monitoring: a pre-registered simulation study

The problem

After trauma, departures from physiological health may develop gradually and at the same time across several systems, such as autonomic arousal, sleep and threat reactivity, while no single measure crosses a clinical threshold. Conventional monitoring checks each measure separately, or checks all of them against population norms. Either way, it ignores the person's own trajectory.

The method

A dynamic factor model with person-level intercepts and a Kalman filter predicts each person's multivariate state one day ahead. Daily surprise is the squared standardized prediction error, split into a coordinated part (movement along shared latent drivers) and a decoupling part (for example, physiological arousal without matching self-report). Evidence then accumulates over time in a cumulative-sum (CUSUM) chart.

Dt = vt⊤St−1vt = T²t + Qt,   Dt ~ χ²(kt) under the null
Illustrative schematic, not results. Threat, arousal and sleep indicators drift slowly from the dashed line. Each stays within its personal baseline band (grey), but evidence accumulated across all of them crosses the alarm threshold.

Simulation design

Each simulated person is observed for 84 days on twelve indicators across four RDoC domains, on realistic schedules: imaging and lab measures twice, questionnaires weekly, and wearables nightly with about 15% of nights missing.

Acute threatStartle · skin conductance · amygdala reactivity · lab distress rating
Sustained threatDiurnal cortisol slope · attentional bias toward threat
ArousalHeart rate variability · resting heart rate · self-reported arousal
SleepWake after sleep onset · total sleep time · insomnia severity

Scenarios

S0 NullChecks the false-alarm rate
S2 Coordinated step+0.5 SD in all threat–arousal indicators from day 30; none individually anomalous
S3 DecouplingPhysiology rises, self-report unchanged
S4 Gradual drift primaryThreat–arousal driver rises 0 → 0.5 SD between days 30 and 72

Methods compared

AUnivariate monitoring of each indicator, with multiplicity correction
BPopulation-referenced Mahalanobis distance, without individual history
D proposedPerson-specific dynamic factor monitoring with surprise decomposition

All arms share the same CUSUM decision rule and are calibrated to the same 5% false-alarm rate on independent null simulations.

Pre-registered hypotheses

H1 · Earlier detection

Under gradual drift, the person-specific method detects at least 7 days earlier (median) than the population-referenced method, with sensitivity no more than 0.02 lower. Both margins must exceed two Monte Carlo standard errors.

H2 · Correct attribution

At least 70% of detected decoupling cases are attributed to the decoupling component, and no more than 30% of coordinated-shift cases are wrongly attributed to it.

Designed and reported under the ADEMP framework (Morris, White & Crowther, 2019). The protocol will be registered on the Open Science Framework before any analysis. Planned outputs include a fully reproducible code repository and an illustrative individual-level alarm report.

Paper in preparation: Hasan, R. U. Detecting Gradual, Coordinated Physiological Drift by Person-Specific Sequential Monitoring: A Pre-Registered Simulation Study.
